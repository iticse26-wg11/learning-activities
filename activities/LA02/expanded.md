# LA02. AI History Timeline — expanded version

In this activity, student groups must arrange a set of 12 ‘milestones’ in the history of artificial intelligence along a chronological timeline, without the aid of research or (much) prior knowledge. These provided milestones are concentrated in the 20th century or earlier and often have (purposefully) vague descriptions, emphasizing the long tail of AI history and connections between more recent developments in the field of AI and recurring themes of automation and conceptions of “intelligence”. The milestones are deliberately chosen rather than exhaustive, representing different approaches to AI, technological achievements, and societal responses to automation. Some examples predate modern AI and are included to encourage discussion about historical concepts. After groups complete their timeline, an opportunity should be given for students to compare their work with other groups' decisions, before the instructor leads a combined class discussion during which the ‘true’ timeline (i.e. the years listed for each milestone below) is revealed. The timeline activity may end with a broader discussion of students' takeaways and surprises, as well as a more formal (and chronological) introduction to key stages in the development of ‘AI’, including periods of rapid advancement and declining expectations known as ‘AI winters’.

## Activity instructions

Arrange the following ‘milestones’ in artificial intelligence on a timeline from 1700 to the 2020s (and beyond) according to when your group thinks the first example appeared or took place.

> **N.B.** For instructor convenience, the milestones below are presented in chronological order with the corresponding ‘solutions’. For the activity, students should be given *only* the titles of the milestones, in a randomized order.

### AI milestones

- **Chess-playing robot**
  - 1770: Wolfgang von Kempelen mystifies European royalty with the “Mechanical Turk”, a machine that moves chess pieces on a board to play against a human opponent. In reality, the robot was controlled by a chess master hidden in a secret compartment below the table. Although not an example of AI, the Mechanical Turk illustrates the long history of attempts to create machines that appear intelligent and the difficulties of distinguishing genuine machine autonomy from human assistance.
- **Widespread coordinated worker protests against industrial automation**
  - 1810s: Textile workers in England protest against factory automation through weaving machines that undermined working conditions and output quality. Named “Luddites” after their fictional leader Ned Ludd, many rioters faced violent repression by industrialists and the state. Although this machinery was not AI, these protests illustrate longstanding concerns about the economic and social consequences of automation.
- **Model of the brain through neural ‘networks’**
  - 1930s-1943: Nicolas Rashevsky develops mathematical models of neurons and nervous activity during the 1930s. Building on earlier mathematical approaches to biological processes, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts publish “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity” in 1943, describing networks of simplified neurons whose behavior can be represented using formal logic. Their work becomes an important foundation for artificial neural networks.
- **Computer that solves problems using logical reasoning**
  - 1956–1957: Allen Newell, John Clifford Shaw, and Herbert A. Simon develop Logic Theorist (1956), an early AI program capable of proving mathematical theorems using symbolic reasoning. The researchers subsequently begin developing the General Problem Solver (1957), which aims to solve a broader range of problems through general problem-solving strategies. These systems represent important early developments in symbolic AI, in which computers manipulate symbols according to explicit rules.
- **High school algebra word problems solver**
  - 1964: Daniel G. Bobrow's PhD thesis project STUDENT uses rule-based logic to process and solve high school-level algebra word problems in ‘natural language’. The system demonstrates early attempts to combine natural-language processing with symbolic problem-solving.
- **Computer ‘chatbot’**
  - 1966: Joseph Weizenbaum releases the ‘chatbot’ system ELIZA, which simulates a psychoanalyst by reflecting user input back in the form of a question. Despite its relatively simple pattern-matching mechanisms, some users attributed human-like understanding to the system.
- **Major decline in AI research funding and expectations**
  - 1970s–1990s: Artificial intelligence experiences periods of reduced research funding, declining public confidence, and disappointment with earlier promises, commonly known as ‘AI winters’. The first major period occurs during the 1970s, while a second follows the decline of the expert-systems industry in the late 1980s and early 1990s. These periods illustrate how technological limitations, unmet expectations, and changing research priorities have shaped AI development.
- **Computer that can beat a chess grandmaster**
  - 1997: IBM's Deep Blue supercomputer defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, using specialized hardware, evaluation methods, and extensive search through possible chess positions. Computers had already defeated grandmasters in individual games, including victories by HiTech and Deep Thought in 1988, but Deep Blue's victory marks the first time a computer defeats the reigning world champion in a match under standard tournament conditions.
- **‘Self-driving’ car that carries passengers without a driver within specific city road systems**
  - 2017–2020: Waymo begins testing fully driverless vehicles without a human safety driver behind the wheel in a defined area of Arizona in 2017. Earlier developments include Google's Firefly self-driving vehicle prototypes, which were tested on public roads with safety drivers in 2015. In 2020, Waymo opens its fully driverless ride-hailing service to the general public in a limited area around Phoenix. These systems demonstrate autonomous driving under specific operational conditions rather than on all roads and in all environments.
- **Multipurpose large language model (LLM)**
  - 2019: OpenAI introduces GPT-2, a transformer-based language model capable of generating coherent text across a wide range of prompts without being designed for only one specific task. Building on the transformer architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017, GPT-2 represents an important stage in the development of general-purpose language models. Subsequent systems, including GPT-3 (2020) and ChatGPT (2022), substantially expand their capabilities and public accessibility.
- **Fully autonomous car capable of driving safely on any road in any part of the world**
  - Future: does not currently exist. This milestone highlights the distinction between AI systems capable of performing successfully in restricted environments and systems capable of operating safely across unrestricted real-world situations.
- **Personal ‘AI milestone’**
  - Reflect on a moment in life that influenced your thinking or understanding about AI.
