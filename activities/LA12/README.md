# AI Evaluation Frameworks and Policies

**Related ILOs:** EPR10, EPR12
**Type:** In-class, Small groups, Unplugged · **Duration:** 90 minutes (45 + 45 for the two parts) · **Scale:** 2 or more small groups of 4–5 students

## Description

The goal of this activity is for students to learn how to evaluate GenAI systems, first with criteria they build themselves, and then with real AI policies, so they can see what formal rules add and what they miss. This activity has two parts: first, students build their own evaluation criteria, and then they apply real AI policies.

**Part 1 — "AI Evaluation Use".** The students in small groups collect definitions of key normative principles, such as fairness, transparency, accountability, safety, robustness, and human agency & oversight. They first draft their own working definitions of their assigned principles, then as a whole class all groups consolidate these into a shared framework (e.g., on a whiteboard or shared poster). Each group then evaluates a different GenAI model or use case proposed by the instructor (e.g., a chatbot in healthcare advice, an image generator, an AI writing assistant used for grading), rating it against each principle and justifying their judgments with concrete evidence or scenarios.

**Part 2 — applying "AI Policy".** Groups receive excerpts from real frameworks and policies (e.g., EU AI Act risk categories, the university's own AI policy, a company's acceptable use policy). Each group evaluates the same or a different model/use case through the lens of its assigned policy: Which rules apply? How would this system be classified? What obligations or restrictions follow? Groups then present and compare outcomes, showing how different policies lead to different evaluations of the same system, and where the crowdsourced classroom framework matches or differs from the real policies.

The closing discussion looks at power and agency: Who wrote each policy, and whose interests does it protect? Who was not at the table? Do the people affected by these AI systems (students, patients, users) have any real choice or control? What would the policy look like if they had written it?

## Keywords

Fairness, Transparency, Accountability, AI policy, Risk classification

## Prerequisites

- Prior knowledge of GenAI and its limitations
- No prior legal or policy knowledge required
- Part 2 depends on Part 1 having established the vocabulary of the principles

## Resources

- Case cards: 4–6 short descriptions of concrete GenAI systems/use cases, written at an appropriate level of detail (purpose, users, data, known failure modes)
- Simplified AI policy handouts: 1–2 pages each (e.g., EU AI Act summary, university AI policy, one industry AI policy) — pre-processed so groups aren't reading raw legal text
- Whiteboard/flipchart or large poster paper for the shared framework; sticky notes and markers
- An evaluation grid template (principles × rating) for groups to fill in

## Assessment

Each group submits its completed evaluation grid, including its definitions and its evaluation using the AI policy.
