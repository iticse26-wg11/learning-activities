# AI Evaluation Frameworks and Policies

**Related ILOs** (at the end of the course, students should be able to …)
- [EPR10](https://github.com/iticse26-wg11/intended-learning-outcomes/blob/main/ilos/EPR10.md): evaluate the use of GenAI systems against relevant normative principles (e.g., fairness, transparency, accountability, safety, robustness, and human governance).
- [EPR12](https://github.com/iticse26-wg11/intended-learning-outcomes/blob/main/ilos/EPR12.md): identify and interpret relevant GenAI policies, assessing their applicability to and impacts on GenAI use in different contexts.

**Type:** In-class, small groups, unplugged · **Duration:** 90 minutes (45 per phase) · **Scale:** Two or more small groups

## Description

The goal of this activity is for students to learn how to evaluate GenAI systems, first with criteria they build themselves, and then with real AI policies, so they can see what formal rules add and what they miss. This activity has two phases: first, students build their own evaluation criteria, and then they apply real AI policies.

*Phase 1: AI Evaluation Use.* Working only from their own knowledge and understanding of GenAI, and without first looking up formal definitions, students in small groups draft working definitions of key normative principles, such as fairness, transparency, accountability, safety, robustness, and human agency & oversight. All groups then consolidate these into a shared framework (e.g., on a whiteboard or shared poster). Each group then evaluates a different GenAI model or use case proposed by the instructor (e.g., a chatbot in healthcare advice, an image generator, an AI writing assistant used for grading), rating it against each principle and justifying their judgments with concrete evidence or scenarios. Keeping this phase to students' own intuitions is intentional, because the activity is built on pointing out the gap between these working definitions and the formal ones introduced in phase 2.

*Phase 2: AI Policy.* During this phase, groups receive excerpts from real frameworks and policies (e.g., EU AI Act risk categories, the university's own AI policy, a company's acceptable use policy). Each group evaluates the same or a different model/use case through the lens of its assigned policy: Which rules apply? How would this system be classified? What obligations or restrictions follow? Once they finish, they compare the definitions they drafted in phase 1 with the definitions of the principles in the formal policies. Each group sets its phase 1 framework side by side with its assigned policy and works through where the two meet, looking for them in its assigned policy: Is the principle actually mentioned at all? If it is, how does the policy define it — the same way we did, or narrower, broader, or framed around different concerns? Does the policy fold in considerations we left out, or leave out things we thought were essential? And are there principles we treated as central (e.g., human agency, dignity) that the policy doesn't recognize as principles at all? Groups then present and compare outcomes, showing how different policies lead to different evaluations of the same system, and where the crowdsourced classroom framework matches or differs from the real policies. The closing discussion looks at power and agency: Who wrote each policy, and whose interests does it protect? Who was not at the table? Do the people affected by these AI systems (students, patients, users) have any real choice or control? What would the policy look like if they had written it?

## Keywords

Fairness, transparency, accountability, AI policy, risk classification

## Prerequisites

- Familiarity with GenAI and its limitations; no legal or policy knowledge required; Phase 2 builds on the vocabulary established in Phase 1

## Resources

- Case cards: 4–6 short descriptions of GenAI systems or use cases (purpose, users, data, known failure modes)
- Simplified policy handouts (1–2 pages, one per group) quoting how each policy defines or omits the Phase 1 principles, e.g., the EU AI Act, the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI with its ALTAI checklist, the university's own AI or academic-integrity policy, or a provider's usage policy
- Poster paper or whiteboard, sticky notes and markers for the shared framework
- Evaluation grid template: principles and ratings, policy classification and obligations, principle-by-principle comparison

## Assessment

Each group submits its completed evaluation grid
